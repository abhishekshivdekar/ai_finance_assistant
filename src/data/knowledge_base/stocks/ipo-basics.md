# IPO Basics

An initial public offering, or IPO, is the moment a privately owned company offers its shares to the general public for the first time. It is also called a new issue or a public issue. The article "What Is a Stock?" mentions IPOs in one line; this article walks through how the process usually works.

## Why a company goes public

A private company is owned by its founders and a limited set of investors. Going public can help it in several ways:

- **Raising money** for expansion, new products or repaying debt.
- **Letting early backers realise value**, since existing owners can sometimes offer part of their holding in the IPO.
- **Raising visibility and credibility**, as listed companies must publish regular financial results.

Going public also brings duties: regular disclosures, public scrutiny and the need to answer to many shareholders.

## The main steps

1. **Choosing advisers.** The company appoints investment banks, often called merchant bankers in India, to manage the offer.
2. **Preparing the offer document.** In India this is a draft document filed with SEBI, and a final version follows. It describes the business, its finances, its risks and how the money will be used. Reading the risk section is a habit many investors build.
3. **Regulatory review.** The regulator checks that the required information is included. Approval is not a statement that the shares are a good or bad choice.
4. **Setting the price.** The company may fix a single price or offer a price band within which investors place bids. The band is the range between a lowest and highest allowed price.
5. **The subscription window.** Investors apply for shares for a few days. If more shares are applied for than are available, the issue is oversubscribed.
6. **Allotment and listing.** Shares are allotted to successful applicants, and then the stock starts trading on exchanges such as the NSE and BSE.

## Fresh issue and offer for sale

An IPO can contain two parts. A **fresh issue** creates new shares, and the money goes to the company. An **offer for sale** lets existing shareholders pass on some of their shares, and the money goes to those sellers, not the company. Many IPOs mix both, so investors often check how the proceeds are split.

## Categories of applicants

Issues in India set aside portions for different groups, such as retail individuals, institutional investors and non-institutional investors. Each group has its own allotment rules, which is why an applicant may receive fewer shares than requested, or none. A beginner who says "I applied but did not get allotted" has usually run into this: when an issue is oversubscribed, allotment can be by lottery, and the unallotted application money is released back to the applicant.

## Listing day and what follows

When the shares first trade, they may open above or below the issue price. The difference between the two is sometimes called a listing gain or loss. Early trading can swing widely as thousands of participants react at once, and an IPO price is no promise about later performance. Some IPO shares have fallen well below their issue price, while others have risen, and the outcome varies from case to case.

## Things investors often examine

- How the company actually earns money and whether profits are steady.
- How much debt it carries.
- What the issue proceeds will be used for.
- How the offer price compares with similar listed firms, for example using the price-to-earnings ratio.

## Key takeaway

An IPO converts a private company into a publicly traded one, using a regulated process of documents, price setting, bidding, allotment and listing. Careful reading of the offer document helps investors understand what they would own. This article is educational and does not suggest participating in any issue.
