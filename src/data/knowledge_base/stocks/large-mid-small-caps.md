# Large-Cap, Mid-Cap and Small-Cap Companies

Listed companies come in very different sizes, and investors group them by market capitalisation. The article "What Is a Stock?" mentions these labels in passing. This article explains how the groups are drawn and how they tend to differ. See the article "Market Capitalisation" for how the size figure itself is calculated.

## How the groups are defined

There is no universal rule. Different countries, index providers and fund houses draw the lines differently. In India, market regulators and the mutual fund industry body use a ranking system rather than fixed rupee amounts: companies are listed by full market cap and sorted by position. Because the ranking is relative, a company can move between groups as markets and other companies change. In the United States, dollar-based ranges are more common, and they are revised from time to time.

## Large caps

These are the biggest, most established listed companies. Common features include:

- Long operating histories and wide customer bases.
- Heavy trading volumes, so shares are easy to trade at close to the displayed price.
- Heavy coverage by analysts and the media, so information is plentiful.
- Moderate growth potential relative to smaller companies, because they are already big.
- Presence in major indices, which means index-linked funds hold them.

Large caps are not free from risk. Even big companies can face falling profits, management failures or industry disruption, and during broad market declines their prices usually fall too.

## Mid caps

Mid-cap companies sit between the two ends. They are often established businesses that are still expanding into new markets or products. Investors often see them as offering more growth potential than large caps, while being less proven. Their shares can swing more than large-cap shares, and trading volumes are lower.

## Small caps

Small-cap companies are the smallest of the three groups. They may be young, focused on a niche or operating in a single region. Typical features include:

- Larger room for growth in percentage terms, since the starting base is small.
- Greater price swings, sometimes within a single day.
- Lower liquidity, which can make trades hard to complete at a desired price.
- Less analyst coverage, so information can be harder to find.
- Greater exposure to the failure of a single product or customer.

## Why the distinction matters

The groups tend to behave differently across market phases. In some periods, smaller companies surge ahead; in others, large caps hold up better. These patterns are not reliable enough to predict, and past behaviour does not guarantee future behaviour.

## Funds and categories

Mutual fund schemes are often labelled by the size of companies they hold, for example a large-cap fund, a mid-cap fund or a multi-cap fund. Knowing the labels helps a reader understand the scheme documents and the type of ups and downs to expect. The articles on ETFs and mutual funds explain how funds work in general.

## Mixing sizes

Many investors hold companies of several sizes to avoid relying on one segment, an application of the diversification idea. The proportions depend on personal goals and comfort with risk, and this article does not suggest any particular split.

## Key takeaway

Large, mid and small caps are size groupings by market capitalisation. Larger companies are generally more established and easier to trade, while smaller ones tend to offer more growth potential alongside more volatility and less liquidity. This article is educational only.

## In everyday words

"What do people mean by big, medium and small companies on the stock market?" These are size groups based on market capitalisation. Larger companies are generally more established and easier to trade, while smaller ones tend to offer more growth potential alongside bigger ups and downs and less ease of trading.
