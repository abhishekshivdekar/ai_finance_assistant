# Stock Indices: Sensex and Nifty

When a news bulletin says "the market rose today", it usually refers to a stock index. An index is a single number that summarises the movement of a chosen group of shares, much like a thermometer summarises temperature.

## What an index is

An index tracks a basket of stocks. Its value is calculated from the prices of the companies in the basket, using a set method and a starting reference point. If most shares in the basket rise, the index rises. An index is not something one can trade directly, but funds and derivatives can be built to follow it.

## Two Indian examples

- **Sensex** is the benchmark index of the BSE. It follows a group of large, well-established companies listed there.
- **Nifty 50** is the flagship index of the NSE. It follows a larger group of leading companies listed on that exchange.

Because both include many of the same big names across banking, technology, energy and consumer goods, they often move in the same direction. Their numbers differ because each has its own starting date, starting value and calculation, so the two levels cannot be compared directly. Each index provider also publishes broader indices covering mid and small companies, as well as sector indices such as banking or information technology.

## How weights work

In most major indices, companies are weighted by free-float market capitalisation. This means a company with a larger value of publicly tradable shares has more influence on the index. As an invented example, if one company makes up a fifth of an index and its price rises, the index moves more than if a tiny company rose by the same amount. The article on market capitalisation explains the size measure behind this.

## Points and percentage change

Index news is quoted in points, such as "the index gained 200 points". A point alone says little, because it depends on the index level. Percentage change is easier to compare across time. An increase of 200 points on a level of 20,000 is one percent. The same 200 points on a level of 60,000 is about a third of a percent. This is invented arithmetic for illustration only.

## Rebalancing

Index providers review their baskets on a schedule. Companies that have grown smaller or no longer meet the rules can be removed, and rising ones can be added. This keeps the index representative of the market, and it also means an index is not the same group of companies forever.

## Why indices matter

- **Benchmarking.** An investor or fund can compare its results against a relevant index to see whether it did better or worse than the market it operates in.
- **Market mood.** The index is a quick gauge of overall sentiment.
- **Index funds and ETFs.** These funds aim to copy an index, usually at a lower cost than actively managed funds. The article on ETFs and mutual funds explains the idea.

## Limits

An index shows the past, not the future. A rising index does not mean every share in it rose, and index returns may differ from what an investor in an index fund receives, because of fund costs and small tracking gaps. Price indices also usually leave out dividends, although total return versions exist.

## Key takeaway

Sensex and Nifty 50 are summary numbers for baskets of large Indian companies, weighted mostly by free-float market value. They help describe how the market is doing and provide benchmarks for funds. This article is educational only.
