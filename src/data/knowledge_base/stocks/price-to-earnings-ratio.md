# Price-to-Earnings Ratio

The price-to-earnings ratio, written P/E, is one of the most quoted numbers in stock market discussions. The article "What Is a Stock?" introduces it in a sentence. Here is a closer look at how it is built, how it is read and where it can mislead.

## The formula

P/E = share price / earnings per share (EPS)

Earnings per share is a company's profit after tax divided by its number of shares. As an invented example, suppose a company earns 10 rupees per share in a year and its shares trade at 200 rupees. The P/E is 20. One way to describe this is that the market is paying 20 rupees for each rupee of the company's yearly profit.

## Trailing and forward P/E

- **Trailing P/E** uses profit from the last twelve months. It is based on reported facts.
- **Forward P/E** uses analysts' estimates of next year's profit. It looks ahead, but estimates can turn out wrong.

When reading any P/E figure, check which version is shown, because the two can differ a lot for a fast-changing business.

## What a high or low figure may suggest

A high P/E can mean investors expect profits to grow quickly, or that the shares are priced richly compared with current profits. A low P/E can mean modest growth expectations, concern about the business, or a lower price compared with profits. The ratio itself does not say which explanation applies. A low number is not automatically a bargain, and a high number is not automatically a warning.

## Comparing sensibly

A P/E is most useful when compared with a fitting benchmark:

- **Companies in the same industry.** Banks, software firms and utilities tend to trade at different typical levels, so comparing across industries can mislead.
- **The company's own history.** Investors sometimes look at whether today's P/E is above or below its past range.
- **A market index.** Exchanges such as the NSE and BSE publish P/E figures for their main indices, which gives a sense of how the overall market is priced.

## When P/E does not work well

- **Negative or tiny earnings.** A company making a loss has no meaningful P/E, and one with very small profits can show an extremely large one.
- **One-off items.** A big asset sale can lift profit for one year and make the P/E look unusually low.
- **Cyclical businesses.** Firms whose profits swing with the economy can look cheapest at their profit peak and most expensive at their trough.
- **Accounting differences.** Companies may report profit in different ways, and debt levels are not captured at all.

## Related ratios

Investors often pair P/E with other measures. **Price-to-book** compares the price with the company's net asset value per share. **Price-to-sales** compares the price with revenue, which helps where profits are absent. **PEG ratio** divides the P/E by an expected growth rate to take growth into account. Each has its own limits.

## Connection to market cap

Since market cap equals share price times shares outstanding, the P/E also equals market cap divided by total yearly profit. The article on market capitalisation covers the size side.

## Key takeaway

The P/E ratio relates a share price to a company's profit per share. It is a starting point for asking questions about how a stock is valued, not an answer by itself, and it works best when compared against similar companies and read with other information. This article is educational only.
