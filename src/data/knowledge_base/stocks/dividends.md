# Dividends Explained

A dividend is a payment a company makes to its shareholders out of its profits. The article "What Is a Stock?" names dividends as one possible source of return. Here we look at how they are declared, paid and measured.

## Who decides

The company's board of directors proposes a dividend, and shareholders often approve the final one at a general meeting. A company has no obligation to pay. Many firms pay regularly, some pay nothing, and some cut or stop payments when business conditions weaken.

## Types of dividend

- **Cash dividend.** Money paid directly into the shareholder's bank account. This is the most common type.
- **Interim and final dividends.** Some companies pay part during the year (interim) and the rest after the year's accounts are approved (final).
- **Special dividend.** A one-time extra payment, perhaps after an unusually good year or the sale of a business unit.
- **Stock dividend.** Extra shares given instead of cash. This is different from a stock split, which just divides existing shares into more pieces without changing the total value.

## Key dates

Four dates matter, and beginners often mix them up:

1. **Declaration date.** The company announces the dividend.
2. **Record date.** The company checks its register to see who the shareholders are.
3. **Ex-dividend date.** To be listed on the record date, a person must already own the share before this day. Holders who acquire the share on or after the ex-date do not receive that dividend. Because of this, the share price commonly drops by about the dividend amount on the ex-date, since the payout has left the company.
4. **Payment date.** The money arrives.

In India, with T+1 settlement at the time of writing, the ex-date and record date fall on the same trading day; check the exchange for the current cycle <!-- VERIFY: with T+1 settlement, the ex-date and record date fall on the same trading day -->.

## Measuring dividends

- **Dividend per share** is the rupee or dollar amount paid for each share.
- **Dividend yield** is the annual dividend per share divided by the current share price. For an invented example, a share priced at 100 rupees that pays 3 rupees a year has a yield of 3 percent. Because the price keeps moving, the yield moves too. A very high yield can sometimes be a sign that the price has fallen sharply because of doubts about the business.
- **Payout ratio** is the share of profit paid out as dividends. A low ratio means the company keeps most profit for reinvestment; a very high one may be hard to sustain.

## Dividends and total return

Dividends are only one part of what a holder earns. Total return combines dividends with price changes. Some holders choose to reinvest dividends into more shares, which over long periods can make a noticeable difference through compounding. Others use them as regular income. Neither approach suits everyone.

## Growth versus income companies

Younger, fast-growing firms often keep their profits to expand, so they may pay little or nothing. Mature firms with steady cash flow more often pay regular dividends. Investors often weigh this when comparing companies, though a dividend is never a guarantee and offers no protection against a falling share price.

## Taxes

Dividends may be taxable, and the rules differ by country and change over time. This article does not give any rates. The official sources for current rules are incometax.gov.in for India and the relevant tax authority elsewhere.

## Key takeaway

A dividend is a discretionary share of profit paid to shareholders. Knowing the key dates, the yield and the payout ratio helps explain announcements and price movements around them. This article is educational only.

## In everyday words

"Does a company have to share its profit with the people who own it?" No. A dividend is a payment out of profits that the company chooses to make, so some firms pay regularly and others pay nothing. Key dates decide who receives a payment, and a dividend never protects against a falling share price.
