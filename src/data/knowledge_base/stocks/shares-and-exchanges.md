# Shares and Stock Exchanges

Once a company has shares in the hands of the public, those shares need a place where people can trade them. That place is a stock exchange. This article looks at how an exchange works behind the scenes. The idea of a share itself is explained in the article "What Is a Stock?".

## What an exchange does

A stock exchange is an organised marketplace with strict rules. Its main jobs are:

- **Bringing buyers and sellers together** in one place, so a holder who wants to exit does not have to search for a counterparty.
- **Showing prices openly.** Everyone can see the latest traded price, which is why exchange prices are often called a public record of what people are willing to pay.
- **Setting listing rules.** A company must meet disclosure and governance standards to have its shares listed, which is meant to give investors reliable information.
- **Making sure trades are completed.** After a deal is matched, the exchange and linked institutions arrange for money and shares to change hands.

## Listed shares and the secondary market

When a company first sells shares to the public, the money goes to the company. This is called the primary market. Almost every trade after that happens in the secondary market, where one investor sells to another. The company does not receive money from these later trades.

## How a trade is matched

Most modern exchanges are electronic. Each participant places an order through a broker, and the exchange computer lines these orders up. Two simple order types are worth knowing:

- A **market order** asks to trade straight away at the best price currently available.
- A **limit order** sets a price boundary, so the trade happens only at that price or better. It may not be filled at all if the market never reaches it.

The gap between the highest price a buyer will pay (the bid) and the lowest price a seller will accept (the ask) is called the spread. Shares that many people trade usually have narrow spreads. Shares that few people trade can have wide spreads, which makes trading them costlier. This ease of trading is called liquidity.

## Brokers, depositories and settlement

Individuals cannot connect to an exchange directly. They use a registered broker. In India, shares are normally held in electronic form in a demat account kept with a depository, so there are no paper certificates. Settlement is the final step in which the buyer receives the shares and the seller receives the money. ## Major exchanges

In India the two main exchanges are the National Stock Exchange (NSE) and the Bombay Stock Exchange (BSE). Many large companies are listed on both. In the United States, the New York Stock Exchange and Nasdaq are the best-known examples.
## Trading hours and regulation

Exchanges trade only during set hours. They are supervised by regulators such as SEBI in India and the SEC in the United States, which set rules against practices like insider trading.

## Key takeaway

A stock exchange is a rule-based marketplace that matches buyers and sellers, publishes prices and helps trades settle. Understanding order types, spreads and liquidity helps explain why the price an investor sees is not always the price a trade is completed at. This article is educational only.
