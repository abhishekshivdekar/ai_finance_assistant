# Market Capitalisation

Market capitalisation, usually shortened to market cap, is a quick measure of how much the stock market values a whole company. The article "What Is a Stock?" gives the one-line formula. This article explains what the number does and does not tell you.

## The calculation

Market cap = current share price x number of shares outstanding.

"Shares outstanding" means the shares held by investors, including company insiders. As an invented example, if a company has 50 crore shares and each trades at 400 rupees, its market cap is 20,000 crore rupees. If the price moves to 440 rupees, the market cap rises to 22,000 crore rupees, even though nothing about the factory or the products changed that day. The figure therefore changes every moment the market is open.

## What it measures

Market cap is the price the market currently puts on the entire ownership of the company. It reflects opinion about the future as well as present facts. It is not the amount of cash the company has, nor the value of its buildings, nor what an acquirer would pay. A buyer taking over a company usually has to offer a premium above the market cap, and also takes on the company's debts.

## Why price alone can mislead

A share priced at 5,000 rupees is not necessarily a bigger company than one priced at 50 rupees. The first might have few shares and the second many. Comparing size requires market cap, not share price. This is a common beginner confusion.

## Free-float market cap

Not all shares are freely traded. Founders, governments and other long-term holders often keep large stakes. **Free-float market cap** counts only the shares available for public trading. Many index providers, including those behind Indian benchmark indices, use free-float values when deciding how much weight each company gets. The article on stock indices covers this further.

## Market cap and enterprise value

Market cap ignores debt and cash. Two firms with equal market caps can be very different if one carries heavy borrowing. **Enterprise value** adds debt and subtracts cash to give a fuller picture of what the whole business is worth. Analysts often use it when comparing companies in the same industry.

## Where market cap is used

- **Size labels.** Companies are grouped as large, mid or small caps. The article on large, mid and small caps explains the categories and why they matter.
- **Index construction.** Larger firms normally carry more weight in a market-cap-weighted index.
- **Fund categories.** Mutual fund schemes may be described by the size of companies they hold.
- **Ratios.** Some valuation ratios use market cap directly, such as market cap divided by sales. The price-to-earnings ratio is closely related, since it equals market cap divided by total profit.

## Limits of the measure

- It moves with sentiment, so a rising market cap shows rising opinion, not necessarily better business results.
- A company can issue new shares and raise its share count, which changes the figure.
- It says nothing about profitability, debt levels or quality of management.
- Different data providers may report slightly different values because of differences in timing or share counts.

## Key takeaway

Market cap is share price multiplied by shares outstanding, giving a snapshot of how the market values a company at one moment. It helps compare company sizes fairly, but it should be read alongside other information such as debt, profits and free float. This article is educational only.
