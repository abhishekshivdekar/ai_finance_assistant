# National Pension System (NPS) Explained

The National Pension System, or NPS, is a voluntary retirement savings scheme in India. Unlike some fixed-rate schemes, the money in NPS is invested in market-linked assets, so the final amount depends on how those investments perform. This article explains the general design in plain language. It gives no tax rates, contribution limits, deduction amounts, deadlines or return figures, because these change. For current details, see incometax.gov.in and the official NPS and pension regulator information, and consider speaking to a qualified adviser for personal questions.

## The basic idea

An NPS subscriber contributes to a personal pension account during working life. The contributions are invested through professionally managed pension funds. When the subscriber reaches retirement, the accumulated amount is used partly to provide a regular income and partly, within the rules, as a lump sum.

## Who it is for

<!-- VERIFY --> NPS is open to many categories of people, including salaried employees, the self-employed and others, within eligibility conditions such as age and citizenship. Some employers also offer contributions on behalf of their staff. Eligibility has been broadened over time, so confirm who can join before relying on this description.

## How the money is invested

NPS contributions can go into different kinds of assets. The broad groups are usually:

- **Equity,** which means shares of companies. It has greater potential for growth and greater swings in value.
- **Corporate debt,** which means bonds issued by companies.
- **Government securities,** which are bonds issued by the government and are generally steadier.

Subscribers often choose between taking control of the mix themselves, within limits, and following an automatic approach where the mix changes with age. In the automatic style, the share of riskier assets typically falls as the subscriber gets older, which reflects the idea that a shrinking time horizon calls for steadier holdings.

## Two kinds of accounts

The scheme has generally been described as having two tiers. One is a restricted pension account meant for retirement, and the other is a more flexible, voluntary savings account. <!-- VERIFY --> The restricted account has limits on early withdrawal, while the flexible one has fewer restrictions but different tax treatment. How these tiers work today should be checked.

## The retirement rules

<!-- VERIFY --> At retirement, a portion of the savings is typically required to be used to buy an annuity, which is a product that pays regular income. Rules about premature exit, the share that may be taken as a lump sum, and what happens to the balance have been revised over time. Anyone considering NPS should read the current regulations.

## Who regulates it

NPS is regulated by the Pension Fund Regulatory and Development Authority, shortened to PFRDA. Pension fund managers are appointed and supervised under its framework, and costs charged by them are regulated.

## Taxation in general terms

NPS has been associated with tax benefits at the contribution stage and with specific rules at the time of withdrawal. Which benefits apply depends on the tax regime a person has chosen and on rules in force that year. More than one regime exists, and incometax.gov.in has the current position.

## Key takeaway

NPS is a market-linked retirement scheme with a defined exit structure, regulated by PFRDA. Its rules and tax treatment change, so confirm current details on official sources. This article is educational and is not tax or investment advice.

## In everyday words

"How is NPS different from PPF?" With NPS your money goes into investments like shares and bonds, so what you end up with depends on how they do, whereas PPF follows a rate the government announces. Part of what you collect at retirement has to be turned into regular income. Because it is meant for old age, getting money out early is limited.
