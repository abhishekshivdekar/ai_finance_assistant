# Capital Gains Basics

When an investment is sold for more than it cost, the profit is called a capital gain. Governments generally tax such profits, and in India the way a gain is treated depends heavily on how long the asset was kept. This article explains the principle in simple terms. It gives no tax rates, exemption amounts, deadlines or date cut-offs, because these have been revised several times and will probably change again. For the current position, see incometax.gov.in, and consider speaking to a qualified tax professional for personal questions.

## What counts as a capital asset

A capital asset is, broadly, something owned for investment or personal use that can be sold for a price. Examples include shares, mutual fund units, bonds, property and gold. Different kinds of assets can be treated differently, so the label matters.

## Gain and loss

A gain arises when the sale value is higher than the purchase cost, with adjustments for certain expenses in some cases. A loss arises when it is lower. Importantly, a gain is only counted for tax when the asset is actually sold or transferred. A rise in price on paper, while the asset is still owned, is called an unrealised gain, and it is generally not taxed until a sale takes place.

An invented example: imagine someone paid a certain amount for shares, and later the sale brought in a larger amount. The difference between the two, after allowed costs, is the capital gain for that sale. No actual figures are given here, because the purpose is only to show the structure.

## Why the holding period matters

The central idea is the **holding period**, the length of time between acquiring an asset and selling it. The tax system divides gains into two groups:

- **Short-term capital gains** arise when the asset is sold relatively soon after it was acquired.
- **Long-term capital gains** arise when the asset has been kept for longer than a threshold period.

<!-- VERIFY --> The threshold that separates short term from long term is not the same for every kind of asset. For example, listed shares, mutual funds, property and gold can each have a different cut-off. Check the current periods on incometax.gov.in.

## How short and long term differ in principle

In principle, the two groups are taxed under different rules. Short-term gains are often taxed in a way that is closer to ordinary income or at a separate special treatment. Long-term gains frequently get a separate treatment, sometimes with an allowance before tax applies, and sometimes with a method that adjusts the purchase cost for inflation for certain assets. Whether such features exist today, and what they look like, depends on current law. This is exactly the kind of detail that has changed in the past, so no specifics are given here.

## STT and equity gains

<!-- VERIFY --> Securities transaction tax, or STT, is a tax collected on certain transactions in listed securities on recognised stock exchanges. It is separate from the tax on the profit itself. The reason it comes up in a capital gains discussion is that the special treatment of gains on listed shares and equity mutual funds can depend on whether this tax was paid on the transaction. Whether STT applies to a particular transaction, and at what rate, is set by law and changes over time, so check the official source for the current position.

## Long-term and short-term capital gains (LTCG and STCG)

<!-- VERIFY --> LTCG stands for long-term capital gains and STCG for short-term capital gains. A gain is long term when the asset was held for longer than the holding period set for that type of asset, and short term when it was sold within that period. The two kinds are taxed under different rules. The holding-period thresholds and the tax rates come from the official source, and this article gives none of them.

## Key takeaway

A capital gain is the profit made on selling an asset, and the holding period decides whether it is treated as short term or long term. Rates, thresholds and allowances change, so confirm them on the official site. This article is educational and is not tax advice.

## In everyday words

"Do I pay tax if I make a profit on my investments?" Generally the profit counts when you actually sell, not while you are still holding the investment. How long you kept it can change which rules apply. The exact numbers change often, so people check the Income Tax Department's website for the latest.
