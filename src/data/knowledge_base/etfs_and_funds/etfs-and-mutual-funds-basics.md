# ETFs and Mutual Funds Basics

Instead of picking individual shares or bonds one by one, many investors use funds. A fund pools money from many people and invests it in a basket of securities, which is managed according to a stated goal. Two of the most common types are mutual funds and exchange-traded funds, or ETFs.

## How a pooled fund works

When an investor puts money into a fund, they receive units. Each unit represents a small slice of everything the fund holds. If the fund holds shares in fifty companies, one unit gives exposure to all fifty. The value of one unit is called the **net asset value (NAV)**. It is the total value of the fund's holdings, minus costs, divided by the number of units.

## Mutual funds

A mutual fund is bought from, and redeemed with, the fund company. In most cases the transaction happens at the NAV calculated at the end of the trading day, rather than at a price that changes minute by minute. Many mutual funds allow regular small contributions, often called systematic investment plans, or SIPs, which let investors add a fixed amount at set intervals.

Mutual funds come in many styles: equity funds that hold shares, debt funds that hold bonds and similar instruments, hybrid funds that mix the two, and money market funds that hold very short-term instruments.

## Exchange-traded funds

An ETF also holds a basket of securities, but its units trade on a stock exchange during market hours, just like shares. The price moves through the day with supply and demand, and it usually stays close to the value of the underlying holdings. Many ETFs aim to follow an index such as a broad market index, which is why they are often described as passive.

## Active and passive management

- **Active funds** have managers who choose holdings in an attempt to beat a benchmark. They typically charge higher fees because of the research involved, and results vary widely.
- **Passive or index funds** try to match the performance of an index rather than beat it. They usually cost less because there are fewer decisions to make.

Neither approach is guaranteed to do better. Investors often compare how a fund has performed against its benchmark over several years, and they remember that past results do not predict the future.

## Costs to understand

The most visible cost is the **expense ratio**, the yearly fee charged by the fund as a percentage of the money invested. For instance, an expense ratio of 1 percent on an investment of 100,000 rupees would be 1,000 rupees a year, taken from the fund's value rather than billed separately. This is an example with made-up numbers. Small differences in fees can add up over many years because they reduce the amount that stays invested and compounds.

Other costs can include entry or exit charges on some funds, brokerage when trading ETFs, and the gap between the buying and selling price, known as the bid-ask spread.

## Things investors often check

- The fund's objective and what it actually holds.
- The expense ratio and any other charges.
- The benchmark and how the fund has tracked or beaten it.
- The size of the fund and how long it has existed.
- The risk level described in its official documents.

Regulators require funds to publish documents describing their strategy and risks. In India, SEBI regulates mutual funds and AMFI publishes industry information.

## Key takeaway

Funds let investors own a diversified basket through a single product. Mutual funds and ETFs differ mainly in how they are bought and priced, and costs matter over time. This article is educational and does not recommend any fund.
