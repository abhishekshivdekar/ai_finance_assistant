# SIP versus Lump Sum Investing

There are two common ways to put money into a fund. A **lump sum** is a single large payment. A **systematic investment plan (SIP)** is a fixed amount invested at regular intervals, such as monthly. The article "ETFs and Mutual Funds Basics" introduces SIPs in one line. This article compares the two approaches and explains how each behaves when prices move.

## How a SIP works

An investor sets up a standing instruction to invest, say, 5,000 rupees on a certain date each month. Each instalment buys units at that day's NAV. When the NAV is lower, the same money buys more units. When the NAV is higher, it buys fewer.

## An invented example

Suppose the NAV of a fund over four months is 100, 80, 90 and 110 rupees. An investor puts in 1,000 rupees each month.

- Month 1 buys 10 units.
- Month 2 buys 12.5 units.
- Month 3 buys about 11.1 units.
- Month 4 buys about 9.1 units.

Total spent is 4,000 rupees and total units are about 42.7, so the average cost per unit is about 93.7 rupees. The simple average of the four NAVs is 95. Because more units were bought when prices were low, the average cost came out a little lower. This effect is often called **rupee cost averaging**. The prices here are made up purely to show the arithmetic.

## What a SIP does and does not do

- It spreads the entry over time, reducing the effect of choosing one unlucky day.
- It builds a habit, since money is invested before it can be spent elsewhere.
- It suits people who earn monthly rather than holding a large amount at once.
- It does not guarantee profit or protect against loss. If prices fall steadily, every instalment buys units that keep losing value.
- It does not make the fund itself less risky.

## How a lump sum behaves

With a lump sum, all the money is invested on one day. If markets rise afterwards, the whole amount benefits from the full period of growth. If markets fall soon after, the entire amount takes the hit. Over long periods, studies in some markets have found lump sums often did better than spreading the same money out, simply because markets tend to rise more often than they fall, but this is not certain and depends on the period chosen.

## Comparing the two

| | SIP | Lump sum |
|---|---|---|
| Timing risk | Spread out | Concentrated on one day |
| Cash needed upfront | Small | Large |
| Behaviour in a steady rise | Later instalments buy at higher prices | Full amount grows from day one |
| Behaviour in a steady fall | Averages down the cost | Full amount falls at once |

## Practical points

- Many funds allow SIPs of small amounts, and the schedule can usually be paused or stopped.
- Each SIP instalment may carry its own exit load clock, as the article "Mutual Fund Exit Loads and Lock-ins" explains.
- Investors sometimes combine both, investing a lump sum in stages.

## Key takeaway

A SIP trades the chance of perfect timing for regular discipline and a smoother entry, while a lump sum puts everything to work at once. Neither is ahead in all conditions. This article is educational only and does not suggest which approach any person should use.
