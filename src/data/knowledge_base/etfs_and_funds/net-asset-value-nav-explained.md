# Net Asset Value (NAV) Explained

Net asset value, or NAV, is the price of one unit of a fund. The article "ETFs and Mutual Funds Basics" defines it in a sentence. This article shows how it is calculated, when it is published, and a common misunderstanding about what a low or high NAV means.

## The calculation

A fund adds up the market value of everything it holds, including shares, bonds and cash. It then subtracts its liabilities and accrued expenses. The result is divided by the number of units outstanding.

NAV = (value of holdings + cash - liabilities and expenses) / number of units

Here is an invented example. A small fund holds securities worth 10.2 crore rupees and cash of 0.3 crore, with 0.1 crore of expenses due. Net assets are 10.4 crore. If 1 crore units are outstanding, the NAV is 10.40 rupees per unit. The numbers are made up to show the steps.

## When it is calculated

Mutual funds calculate NAV once each business day, after the market closes, using closing prices of the holdings. In India, fund houses publish it, and AMFI's site collects daily NAVs for the industry. Money invested or redeemed is processed at the NAV of the applicable day, depending on cut-off times. A purchase made after the cut-off usually gets the next day's NAV.

ETFs also have an end-of-day NAV, but their units trade at market prices during the day, so the price paid may differ slightly from NAV.

## What changes the NAV

- **Price changes in the holdings.** The main driver, up or down.
- **Income received.** Dividends and interest add to the fund's assets.
- **Expenses.** The daily deduction for the expense ratio lowers it slightly.
- **Payouts.** When a fund pays out a dividend under an income distribution option, the NAV falls by about the amount paid, since that money has left the fund.

New investments or redemptions do not change the NAV. When someone puts money in, the fund receives cash and issues matching new units, so the value per unit is unchanged.

## A common misunderstanding: low NAV does not mean cheap

Beginners sometimes believe a fund with an NAV of 12 rupees is a bargain compared with one at 400 rupees. That is not how it works. Suppose an investor puts 10,000 rupees in each. The first gives about 833 units, the second gives 25 units. If both holdings rise by 10 percent, both investments become 11,000 rupees. What matters is the percentage change in NAV, not the starting figure. This is again an invented illustration.

A new fund often starts at a standard unit price, such as 10 rupees, which says nothing about how good it is.

## Using NAV to track a holding

Investors can multiply their units by the current NAV to see what their holding is worth. Comparing the NAV figures of two different funds is not meaningful, though. Comparing a fund's percentage change over time with its benchmark is more informative.

## Where to find it

AMFI publishes daily NAVs for mutual funds, and each fund house shows its own on its website and statements.

## Key takeaway

NAV is simply net assets divided by units. It moves with the value of the holdings, and its level on its own says nothing about whether a fund is cheap or expensive. This article is educational only.
