# Active versus Passive Investing

Funds are often described as active or passive. The article "ETFs and Mutual Funds Basics" gives a short definition of each. This article looks at the thinking behind the two styles, the debate between them, and why the choice is not a simple one.

## The core idea of each style

**Active investing** means a manager researches companies and chooses holdings, trying to produce returns above a chosen benchmark. The manager decides what to hold, when to trade and how much cash to keep.

**Passive investing** means holding the securities of an index in the same proportions, accepting the market's return rather than trying to beat it. Trading happens mainly when the index changes.

## The argument for passive

Supporters of passive investing point to a piece of arithmetic. Before costs, all investors together earn the market's return. Active managers trade against one another, so for every investor who beats the market, another must fall behind it. After subtracting the higher fees and trading costs of active funds, the average active fund must trail the average passive one. Studies in several markets, including reports comparing funds to their benchmarks, have found that a large share of active funds lag their index over long periods. ## The argument for active

Supporters of active investing say that some markets are less efficient than others. In areas with limited information, such as small companies, a skilled manager may find mispriced shares. Active managers can also hold cash in a downturn, avoid parts of an index they consider risky, or respond to events. Also, an index may be heavily weighted to a few large firms, and some investors prefer to look beyond them.

## Costs and consistency

Active funds usually charge a higher expense ratio, and the article "The Expense Ratio Explained" shows how fee gaps compound. The harder question is consistency. A fund that beat its benchmark for three years may not do so for the next three. Past results are not a reliable guide, and it is difficult to tell skill from luck in advance.

## A simple comparison

| | Active | Passive |
|---|---|---|
| Goal | Beat the benchmark | Match the benchmark |
| Decisions | Made by manager | Made by index rules |
| Typical cost | Higher | Lower |
| Key risk | Manager underperforms | Market falls with the index |
| Result relative to index | Can be above or below | Close to the index, minus costs |

## Blending the two

Some investors do not choose one style. They may use passive funds for broad market exposure and add active funds in areas where they believe skill can matter. Others pick one style for simplicity. Both are common, and each carries trade-offs.

## What the choice does not change

Neither style removes market risk. A passive equity fund still falls when the market falls, and an active one can fall further. Diversification, time horizon and costs affect results for both.

## Key takeaway

Active funds try to beat the market at a higher cost, while passive funds accept the market's return at a lower cost. Evidence suggests beating the market consistently is hard, but that does not settle the question. This article is educational and does not recommend either style.
