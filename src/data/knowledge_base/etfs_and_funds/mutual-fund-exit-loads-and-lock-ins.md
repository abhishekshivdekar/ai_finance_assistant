# Mutual Fund Exit Loads and Lock-ins

The article "ETFs and Mutual Funds Basics" explains what a mutual fund is. This article looks at a practical question beginners often ask: once money is in a mutual fund, how easily and how cheaply can it come out again? Two features shape the answer, the exit load and the lock-in.

## Redemption: taking money out

Selling units back to a mutual fund is called **redemption**. In an open-ended fund, the investor sends a redemption request, and the fund pays out at the net asset value (NAV) applicable on the day the request is accepted, usually after a cut-off time. The money normally reaches the bank account after a short processing period that depends on the type of fund.

Redemption is possible for most open-ended funds on any business day. That flexibility is one reason many investors like funds, but it comes with some conditions, described below.

## What is an exit load?

An **exit load** is a fee charged by a fund when units are redeemed within a certain period after purchase. It is deducted from the redemption amount. The purpose is usually to discourage very short-term in-and-out trading, which can disturb the fund's other investors.

Here is an invented example. Suppose a fund states an exit load of 1 percent if units are redeemed within one year. An investor redeems units worth 50,000 rupees after six months. The load would be 500 rupees, so about 49,500 rupees would be paid out. Had the same units been redeemed after the stated period, no load would apply. These numbers are made up purely for illustration; real funds set their own periods and percentages.

Some points investors often note:

- Not every fund charges an exit load, and some charge none at all.
- Where regular investments such as SIPs are made, each instalment is generally treated as a separate purchase with its own clock.
- Exit loads are separate from the yearly expense ratio.

## What is a lock-in?

A **lock-in** is a period during which units cannot be redeemed at all. It is stricter than an exit load, which only adds a cost. Some tax-saving fund categories in India carry a mandatory lock-in, and certain solution-oriented or closed-ended funds do too. Because the rules for tax-saving products change, investors often check incometax.gov.in for the current position <!-- VERIFY: which fund categories carry a mandatory lock-in is a rule that may change -->.

## Why this matters

Money placed in a fund with a lock-in or a high exit load is less flexible. Investors often match the fund's terms to when they expect to need the money, and they avoid assuming that every fund can be exited freely and without cost.

## Rules vary by scheme

There is no single rule that applies to all mutual funds. Exit loads, their durations, lock-in periods and cut-off times differ from scheme to scheme and can be changed by the fund house within regulatory limits. The reliable places to check are the scheme's official documents, such as the scheme information document and the key information memorandum, along with the fund house's website. AMFI and SEBI publish general investor information, but the terms of a specific scheme live in its own documents.

## Key takeaway

Exit loads add a cost to early withdrawals, while lock-ins block withdrawals for a set time. Both are scheme-specific, so the scheme documents are the source of truth. This article is educational only and does not recommend any fund.

## In everyday words

"Can I take my money out of a fund whenever I want, and will it cost me?" It depends on the scheme. An exit load adds a cost to an early withdrawal, and a lock-in blocks withdrawals for a set time. The scheme documents are the source of truth for both.
