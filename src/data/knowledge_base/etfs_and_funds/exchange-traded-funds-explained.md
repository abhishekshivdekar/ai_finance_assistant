# Exchange-Traded Funds Explained

An exchange-traded fund, or ETF, is a fund whose units trade on a stock exchange. The article "ETFs and Mutual Funds Basics" compares ETFs with mutual funds in broad terms. This article goes deeper into how an ETF works behind the scenes and what that means for the person using one.

## Two markets, one fund

An ETF effectively lives in two places. In the **primary market**, large institutions called authorised participants or market makers can create or cancel units by exchanging a basket of the underlying securities with the fund house. In the **secondary market**, ordinary investors buy and sell those units on the exchange through a broker, just as with a share.

This design is what keeps the ETF's price near the value of what it holds. If the unit price drifts well above the value of the underlying basket, institutions can step in, create new units and sell them, which tends to push the price back down. If it falls below, the reverse can happen.

## Price, NAV and iNAV

An ETF has a net asset value, but investors usually see two numbers during the day:

- **Market price.** What the unit is trading at on the exchange right now.
- **Indicative NAV (iNAV).** An estimate, updated through the day, of what the underlying holdings are worth.

The small gap between market price and the value of holdings is called a premium or discount. In busy, well-traded ETFs it is usually tiny. In thinly traded ones it can be larger.

## Costs of using an ETF

- **Expense ratio.** The yearly fund fee, often low for ETFs that follow broad indices.
- **Brokerage.** Charged by the broker on each trade.
- **Bid-ask spread.** The gap between the price buyers offer and sellers ask. Wide spreads make trading more expensive.
- **Tracking difference.** How far the ETF's return falls from the index it follows.

## What an investor needs to hold one

Because ETF units trade on an exchange, an investor needs a demat account and a trading account with a broker. Regular monthly investing is possible but is usually done manually or through broker features, since ETFs do not work exactly like fund-house SIPs.

## Types of ETFs

Many ETFs follow equity indices, but others hold gold, government securities or short-term debt, and some follow a theme or a group of factors. Investors often read the fund's documents to see exactly what the underlying index or asset is, and how it is built.

## Liquidity: a point worth checking

The ability to trade at any time during market hours does not guarantee that trading is easy. If few people trade a particular ETF, a large order may move the price or be hard to complete. Investors often look at average daily trading volume and the spread before relying on an ETF's liquidity.

## Risks to keep in mind

- **Market risk.** An equity ETF falls when its index falls.
- **Tracking error and difference.** The return may not match the index exactly.
- **Concentration.** A narrow-theme ETF can hold only a few industries.
- **Closure.** Funds can be wound up if they stay too small.

## Key takeaway

An ETF combines a fund's diversified basket with the trading convenience of a share. Its price stays near the value of its holdings through a creation and redemption process, but costs such as spreads and tracking difference still matter. This article is educational and does not recommend any ETF.
