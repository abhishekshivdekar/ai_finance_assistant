# Credit Ratings

A bond is only as dependable as the borrower behind it. Credit ratings are a standard way to describe how likely an issuer is to meet its payment promises. The article "How Bonds Work" lists credit risk as one of the main risks; this article explains how ratings are produced and read.

## What a rating is

A credit rating is an opinion, expressed in letters, about an issuer's ability to pay interest and repay principal on time. It is given by a credit rating agency, an organisation that studies the borrower's finances. In India, rating agencies are registered and supervised by SEBI. Well-known international agencies publish ratings for issuers around the world.

## What agencies look at

- The borrower's earnings and cash flows compared with its debts.
- How much it has already borrowed.
- The strength and outlook of its industry.
- The quality of management and past payment record.
- For a specific bond, what claims the bondholders have if things go wrong, for example whether the loan is backed by assets.

## The letter scales

Each agency has its own symbols, but the general pattern is similar. Ratings run from the top, often written as AAA, down through AA, A, BBB, BB, B and lower grades, ending in D for default. Many scales add plus or minus signs, or numbers, to show finer steps. Agencies publish a guide explaining exactly what each symbol means, and Indian ratings are often labelled differently from global ones, so it is worth checking which scale is being used.

## Investment grade and speculative grade

A common dividing line separates the scale into two groups:

- **Investment grade** covers the upper bands (roughly BBB and above on global scales). These issuers are seen as having a relatively lower chance of default.
- **Speculative grade** covers the lower bands. Issuers here are seen as having a higher chance of default, and are sometimes called high-yield or junk bonds.

Some institutions, such as certain funds, are limited by their own rules to holding mainly investment-grade bonds.

## Ratings and yield

Issuers seen as riskier usually have to offer a higher yield to attract lenders. So the yield on a lower-rated bond tends to be higher than on a higher-rated one of the same maturity. The extra yield is called the credit spread. A wide spread suggests the market sees more risk. The article "Coupon and Yield" explains yield measures.

## Rating changes and outlooks

Ratings are not fixed. An agency may upgrade or downgrade a bond as the borrower's position changes. It may also give an **outlook** (positive, stable or negative) or place a rating under review. A downgrade often pushes the price of existing bonds lower, because the market now demands a higher yield for the higher risk.

## Limits of ratings

- A rating is an opinion, not a guarantee. Highly rated issuers have occasionally failed.
- Ratings can lag behind events, since agencies update them only periodically or after news.
- Ratings cover credit risk only. They say nothing about interest rate risk, which the article on bond risks discusses.

## Key takeaway

Credit ratings express, in letters, an agency's opinion on how likely a borrower is to pay as promised. Higher grades suggest lower credit risk and typically lower yields; lower grades suggest higher risk and higher yields. They are helpful but imperfect. This article is educational only.
