# How Bonds Work

A bond is a loan that an investor makes to a borrower, usually a government or a company. In return, the borrower promises to pay regular interest and to give back the original amount on a fixed date. Because the terms are set in advance, bonds are often called fixed-income investments.

## The basic parts of a bond

- **Issuer.** The government or company borrowing the money.
- **Face value (or par value).** The amount that will be repaid at the end. Many bonds are issued with a face value such as 1,000 or 100 units of currency.
- **Coupon.** The interest the bond pays, usually stated as a percentage of the face value each year. A bond with a face value of 1,000 rupees and a 6 percent coupon would pay 60 rupees a year. This is an invented example for illustration.
- **Maturity date.** The day the issuer repays the face value. Short-term bonds may mature within a year or two, while long-term bonds can run for ten, twenty or thirty years.

## Types of issuers

- **Government bonds** are issued by national governments. They are generally seen as having a lower chance of default, especially when issued in the country's own currency.
- **Corporate bonds** are issued by companies. They usually offer a higher coupon than government bonds to compensate lenders for taking more risk.
- **Municipal or state bonds** are issued by local authorities in some countries to fund public projects.

## Why bond prices move

After a bond is issued, it can often be traded between investors. Its price then changes with market conditions. The most important relationship is this: when interest rates in the economy rise, the prices of existing bonds typically fall, and when rates fall, existing bond prices typically rise. The reason is simple. If new bonds pay higher interest, an older bond with a lower coupon becomes less attractive, so its price must drop to compete.

Investors use the word **duration** to describe how sensitive a bond is to interest rate changes. Longer-maturity bonds usually have higher duration, so their prices tend to move more.

## Yield: a bond's return measure

The coupon is fixed, but the return an investor actually earns depends on the price paid. **Yield** expresses the return relative to the price. If a bond is bought below its face value, the yield is higher than the coupon. If it is bought above face value, the yield is lower. **Yield to maturity** estimates the total annual return if the bond is held until it is repaid and all payments are made.

## Main risks

- **Credit risk.** The issuer may be late or unable to pay. Rating agencies publish credit ratings to help investors judge this risk, with higher ratings suggesting a lower chance of default.
- **Interest rate risk.** As described above, rising rates can reduce the market price of a bond.
- **Inflation risk.** If prices in the economy rise faster than the bond's interest, the real purchasing power of the returns shrinks.
- **Liquidity risk.** Some bonds are hard to trade quickly at a fair price.

## Where bonds fit in a portfolio

Investors often use bonds to bring steadier income and lower price swings than stocks typically show, although bonds are not risk-free and can lose value. Many people hold a mix of stocks and bonds, and the balance usually reflects their goals, time horizon and comfort with ups and downs. The right mix differs from person to person, which is why this article does not point to a specific allocation.

## Key takeaway

A bond is a loan with a promised interest rate and repayment date. Its price moves in the opposite direction to interest rates, and its safety depends on the borrower. Understanding coupon, maturity, yield and credit quality gives a good foundation for reading any bond's description. This is educational material only.

## In everyday words

"What exactly is a bond?" It is a loan that an investor makes to a government or a company. In return, the borrower promises regular interest and to hand back the original amount on a fixed date. The bond's price moves in the opposite direction to interest rates, and how safe it is depends on the borrower.
