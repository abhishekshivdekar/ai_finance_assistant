# Coupon and Yield

Two numbers come up whenever bonds are described: the coupon and the yield. They sound similar and are often confused. The article "How Bonds Work" introduces both briefly; this article separates them and shows the different yield measures with simple examples.

## Coupon: the promise printed on the bond

The coupon is the fixed interest rate a bond pays, stated as a percentage of its face value. It is set when the bond is issued and normally does not change. Many bonds pay the coupon in instalments, such as every six months. A bond with a face value of 1,000 rupees and a 7 percent coupon pays 70 rupees a year, whatever its market price turns out to be. These figures are invented for illustration.

## Current yield: income compared with price

Bonds that already exist trade in the market at prices that can be above or below face value. The **current yield** relates the annual coupon income to the price actually paid:

Current yield = annual coupon / current market price

If the bond above is available at 950 rupees, the current yield is 70 / 950, about 7.4 percent. If it costs 1,050 rupees, the current yield is about 6.7 percent. The coupon stayed the same; only the price paid changed. This is why a person who pays less for the same stream of payments earns a higher yield.

## Yield to maturity: the fuller picture

Current yield ignores the fact that a bond bought below face value will be repaid at full face value, giving an extra gain at maturity, while one bought above face value will lose that difference. **Yield to maturity (YTM)** accounts for all of this. It is the single annual rate that makes the present value of all the remaining coupons and the final repayment equal to the price paid. Most people use a calculator or the figure shown on a trading platform.

YTM carries assumptions: the bond is held until maturity, the issuer pays every instalment on time, and the coupons are reinvested at the same rate. If any of these fail, the actual outcome differs.

## Premium, par and discount

- A bond priced above face value trades at a **premium**; its yield is below its coupon rate.
- A bond priced at face value trades at **par**; its yield equals its coupon rate.
- A bond priced below face value trades at a **discount**; its yield is above its coupon rate.

## Yield to call and zero-coupon bonds

Some bonds let the issuer repay early, which is called a call. **Yield to call** estimates the return if that happens on the earliest permitted date. Zero-coupon bonds, by contrast, pay no regular interest at all. They are issued at a discount and repaid at face value, so the whole return comes from that price gap. Treasury bills in many countries work this way, as described in the article on government securities and treasury bills.

## Why yields move

A rising price means a falling yield, and a falling price means a rising yield. The article "Bond Prices and Interest Rates" explains this see-saw.

A higher yield often reflects a higher risk of the issuer failing to pay, which the article on credit ratings explains.

## Key takeaway

The coupon is the fixed interest promise on a bond, while yield describes the return relative to the price paid. Current yield is a simple snapshot, yield to maturity is a fuller estimate, and neither guarantees the final result. This article is educational only.
