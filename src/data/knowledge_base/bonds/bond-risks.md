# Bond Risks

Bonds are often described as steadier than stocks, but steadier does not mean safe. The article "How Bonds Work" names four main risks in a few lines. This article takes each further and adds some that beginners rarely hear about.

## Interest rate risk

When market rates rise, the prices of existing bonds typically fall, and the longer the maturity, the larger the fall. This matters most to someone who needs to exit before maturity. It also affects bond funds, whose value is recalculated every day and can show losses in periods of rising rates. The article "Bond Prices and Interest Rates" shows the reasoning with numbers.

## Credit risk

The issuer might pay late, pay only part, or fail to pay at all. This is credit risk, or default risk. Companies carry more of it than governments borrowing in their own currency, and weaker companies carry more than stronger ones. Credit ratings, described in "Credit Ratings", offer one view, but a rating can be wrong or slow to change. When a borrower's finances worsen, bond prices can fall before any payment is missed. In a default, bondholders are usually paid ahead of shareholders, but the amount recovered can still be far below what was lent.

## Inflation risk

If prices rise faster than a bond's interest, the real purchasing power of both the interest and the final repayment shrinks. A fixed coupon of a given size buys less each year when inflation is high. Longer bonds are more exposed because their payments stretch further into the future. Some governments issue inflation-linked bonds to address this, but these have their own features and risks.

## Liquidity risk

Some bonds trade rarely. A holder who needs to exit may find few buyers, or only buyers offering a low price. The gap between the buying and selling price (the spread) is often wider for less-traded bonds. Government securities are usually easier to trade than many corporate issues.

## Reinvestment risk

Coupons and repayments received must be put to work again. If market rates have fallen in the meantime, the new money earns less than the original bond did. This risk is greater for bonds with high coupons or those that can be repaid early.

## Call risk

Some bonds give the issuer the right to repay early. Issuers tend to use this right when rates have dropped, since they can borrow more cheaply elsewhere. The holder then receives the money back at the time when reinvestment options are poorer.

## Concentration risk

Holding the bonds of just one issuer places the outcome on a single borrower.

## Risks in bond funds

A bond fund has no single maturity date, so the "hold until the end and get face value back" logic does not apply the same way. The fund's value moves with market prices, and investors in the fund can see losses even when no bond in it defaults. Fund documents describe the fund's average maturity and credit quality, which indicate its level of risk.

## Managing risk in general terms

Investors often respond to these risks by spreading across issuers, maturities and credit qualities, and by matching maturities to the dates when money will be needed. The right approach varies from person to person, and this article does not point to one.

## Key takeaway

Bond risks include interest rate, credit, inflation, liquidity, reinvestment and call risk. Knowing which ones apply to a particular bond makes its yield easier to interpret: a higher return usually reflects more of these risks. This article is educational only.

## In everyday words

"If bonds are steadier than stocks, does that make them safe?" Steadier does not mean safe. Bonds carry several risks, such as changing interest rates, a borrower failing to pay, rising prices eating into returns, difficulty selling, and early repayment by the issuer. A higher return on a bond usually reflects more of these risks.
