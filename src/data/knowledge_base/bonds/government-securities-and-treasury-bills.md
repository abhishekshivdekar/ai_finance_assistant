# Government Securities and Treasury Bills

Governments borrow money by issuing debt, and this debt is among the largest parts of the bond market. In India, such instruments are called government securities, or G-Secs. The article "How Bonds Work" mentions government bonds as one issuer type; this article looks at them more closely.

## Why governments issue debt

Government spending can be larger than tax collection in a given year. The shortfall is covered by borrowing from the public, banks, insurers and other institutions. The borrowing is done by selling securities that promise interest and repayment. In India, the Reserve Bank of India manages the government's borrowing programme and the issuance process.

## Two main families

- **Treasury bills (T-bills)** are short-term instruments that mature in under a year. They pay no regular coupon. Instead, they are issued at a discount to face value and repaid at face value. As a simple invented example, a bill with face value 100 might be issued at 97 and repaid at 100, and the difference is the return. Indian T-bills are issued with a few standard maturities.
- **Government bonds, also called dated securities,** have longer maturities, often ranging from several years to several decades. They normally pay a fixed coupon at regular intervals and repay the face value at maturity. Some governments also issue bonds with floating rates or with payments linked to inflation.

## State government securities

Indian state governments also borrow through securities, often called State Development Loans. They work like central government bonds but carry the state's name as issuer, and their yields are usually a little different from those on central securities.

## Why they are often called low credit risk

A government that borrows in its own currency can, in principle, raise taxes or create currency to repay, so the chance of failing to pay in that currency is generally seen as low compared with companies. That is why yields on government securities are often used as a "risk-free" reference rate in finance. The phrase should be read carefully: it describes credit risk only. Government securities still carry interest rate risk, as the article "Bond Prices and Interest Rates" describes, and inflation risk. Governments have also defaulted on debt in history, particularly when they borrowed in foreign currencies.

## How individuals can access them

The RBI has set up routes for retail participation in government securities, including an online platform for direct holding. Government securities can also be held indirectly through mutual funds or exchange-traded funds that invest in them. Details such as minimum amounts and procedures change, so the RBI website is the proper source.

## The yield curve

Plotting yields of government securities against their maturities gives the yield curve. It usually slopes upward, since lenders ask more for tying up money longer. When short-term yields exceed long-term ones, the curve is called inverted, which analysts sometimes watch as a signal about economic expectations. The curve also serves as a benchmark when pricing corporate bonds, which are often described as trading a certain spread above government yields.

## Liquidity

Government securities are among the most actively traded debt instruments, so they are generally easier to trade than most corporate bonds, although liquidity differs between individual issues.

## Key takeaway

Treasury bills are short-term, discount-priced government debt, and government bonds are longer-term instruments paying coupons. Their credit risk is generally seen as low, but they are still affected by interest rate and inflation movements. This article is educational only.

## In everyday words

"How does the government borrow money from ordinary people?" It issues debt. Treasury bills are short-term and sold at a discount, while government bonds run for longer and pay a coupon. Their credit risk is generally seen as low, but their prices still move with interest rates and inflation.
