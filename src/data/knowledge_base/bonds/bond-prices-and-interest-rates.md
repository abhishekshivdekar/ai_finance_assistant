# Bond Prices and Interest Rates

The article "How Bonds Work" states the key rule: when interest rates rise, existing bond prices tend to fall. This article works through why that happens with a numerical walk-through, and what influences interest rates in the first place. All numbers are invented for illustration.

## A worked example

Suppose a bond was issued at a face value of 1,000 rupees with a 6 percent coupon, so it pays 60 rupees a year. A year later, the market changes: new bonds of similar quality and maturity now pay 8 percent, or 80 rupees on 1,000 rupees.

Who would want the older bond that pays only 60 rupees? Only someone who gets it at a lower price. The price falls until the 60 rupees of annual interest, plus the eventual repayment of face value, looks as attractive as the new bonds. At that lower price the older bond's yield rises to roughly match 8 percent.

Now reverse the story. If new bonds pay only 4 percent, the older 6 percent bond is worth more than before, and its price rises above 1,000 rupees. The yield falls to match the new market level. That is the see-saw: price and yield move in opposite directions.

## Why the price drop is temporary for a holder to maturity

If a bondholder keeps the bond until it matures and the issuer pays everything promised, the face value is returned regardless of the price in between. The price fall is therefore a paper change, shown in the market value. It becomes a real loss only if the bond has to be sold at the lower price, or if the money could have earned more elsewhere in the meantime.

## Duration: how sensitive a bond is

Not all bonds react equally. **Duration** is a measure, in years, of how much a bond's price tends to change when rates change. Broadly:

- Longer maturity usually means higher duration and larger price swings.
- A higher coupon usually means lower duration, because more of the money comes back sooner.

As a rough rule, a bond with a duration of five years might change price by about five percent for each one percentage point change in rates, in the opposite direction. This is a simplification and works best for small rate moves.

## What drives interest rates

- **Central bank policy.** The Reserve Bank of India sets a policy rate that influences borrowing costs across the economy. Other central banks do the same in their own countries.
- **Inflation.** When prices rise quickly, lenders ask for higher interest to protect their purchasing power.
- **Economic growth and demand for credit.** Strong borrowing demand tends to push rates up.
- **Government borrowing.** Heavy borrowing by governments can affect the yields on government securities.

## Reinvestment and the other side

Rising rates are not purely negative. Money returning from maturing bonds can be reinvested at higher yields, and newly issued bonds pay more. Falling rates work the other way: existing holders see price gains, but new money earns less. Which effect matters more depends on the time horizon.

## Key takeaway

Bond prices and interest rates move in opposite directions because a bond's fixed coupon is compared with what new bonds pay. Longer-duration bonds move more. Understanding this helps explain why a bond fund or bond holding can show a loss when central banks raise rates, even without any default. This article is educational only.

## In everyday words

"Why does my bond or bond fund lose value when interest rates go up?" A bond's coupon is fixed, so when new bonds start paying more, the older bond looks less attractive and its price drops. It works the other way too when rates fall. Bonds that run for longer tend to move more, and the loss can show up even though the borrower never missed a payment.
