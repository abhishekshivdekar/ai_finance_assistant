# Risk Tolerance: Willingness Versus Ability

Risk tolerance describes how much ups and downs in the value of savings a person can live with. It is a common topic in planning conversations, and it is often misunderstood as a single feeling. In practice it has two separate parts: the **willingness** to take risk and the **ability** to take risk. This article explains both and why they can disagree.

## Willingness: how it feels

Willingness is emotional and psychological. It asks how someone feels when the value of an investment drops. One person may shrug at a fall of one-fifth and carry on. Another may lose sleep over a much smaller fall.

Willingness is shaped by personality, past experience, and knowledge. Someone who lived through a market crash may feel differently from someone who has only seen rising markets. Planners sometimes use questionnaires, or ask how a person reacted in the past, to get a sense of this side. Answers can change with mood and recent news, so willingness is a little slippery.

## Ability: what the finances can absorb

Ability is practical and numerical. It asks whether a person's finances could withstand a loss without damaging their life. Factors often considered include:

- **Time horizon.** More years until the money is needed usually means more capacity to recover from falls.
- **Stability of income.** A secure, growing income can cover costs even if investments fall.
- **Savings cushion.** A healthy emergency fund means a loss does not force a withdrawal.
- **Dependants and obligations.** Loan instalments, school fees and family responsibilities reduce room for error.
- **Size of the goal.** If a goal is essential, a shortfall matters more than if it is a nice-to-have.

## When the two disagree

Willingness and ability do not always match, and the gaps are where trouble often appears.

Consider an invented example. A young professional with a stable job and no dependants may have a high ability to take risk, but feel nervous and have low willingness. Another person close to retirement, relying on savings for daily costs, may feel confident after years of strong markets, so high willingness, but have low ability, since a large fall could not be made up with future income.

Many planners say the lower of the two is a sensible guide, because a portfolio that is too bold for the finances can cause real harm, and one that is too bold for the person's temperament may be abandoned at the worst moment. Selling in panic after a fall is a well-known way for temporary losses to become permanent.

## Not a fixed label

Terms like conservative, moderate and aggressive are convenient shorthand, but they are rough. A person can be cautious about retirement money and relaxed about a small, long-term fund. Tolerance also shifts with age, income, family changes and experience, so it is worth revisiting from time to time.

## How it fits with other ideas

Risk tolerance works alongside the time horizon and the goal itself. Together they shape asset allocation, which is the way savings are divided among different kinds of investments. No questionnaire can predict how someone will behave in a real downturn, so it is only one input.

## Key takeaway

Risk tolerance has two halves: willingness, which is about feelings, and ability, which is about finances. They can differ, and understanding both gives a clearer picture. This article is educational and is not personal advice.

## In everyday words

People often ask, "How much risk am I okay with?" There are really two questions inside it. One is whether you can stay calm when your money drops. The other is whether your life could cope with that loss, for example paying bills without those savings. You can be bold in one and careful in the other.
