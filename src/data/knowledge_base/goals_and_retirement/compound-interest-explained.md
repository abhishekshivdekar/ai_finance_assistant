# Compound Interest: How Growth Builds on Growth

Compound interest is one of the most talked-about ideas in personal finance, and for good reason. It explains why the same amount of money can end up very different depending on how long it is left to grow. This article describes the idea in plain terms, with invented numbers to show how it works.

## Simple versus compound

With **simple interest**, interest is calculated only on the original amount, called the principal. With **compound interest**, interest is calculated on the principal plus any interest already earned. In other words, interest begins to earn interest of its own.

An invented example makes the difference clear. Imagine 10,000 rupees earning 10 per cent a year. These numbers are made up for illustration only.

- With simple interest, each year adds 1,000 rupees. After three years the total is 13,000.
- With compounding, year one adds 1,000 and the balance becomes 11,000. Year two adds 1,100, making 12,100. Year three adds 1,210, making 13,310.

The gap of 310 rupees looks small after three years, but it keeps widening because each year's growth is calculated on a larger base.

## Why time matters most

The longer money compounds, the more dramatic the effect becomes. In the invented example above, continuing the same growth for thirty years would turn 10,000 into a figure many times larger than the simple-interest version. Real returns are never this steady, so the example should be read as a picture of the mechanism and not a forecast.

Because of this, people often say that starting early matters more than starting big. Someone who begins small and continues for decades can end up with a result that someone who begins later, with larger amounts, struggles to match.

## How often interest is added

Interest can be added, or compounded, yearly, quarterly, monthly or daily. More frequent compounding gives a slightly higher result at the same stated rate. For bank deposits and savings schemes, the compounding frequency is usually stated in the terms, and comparing products fairly requires checking it.

## The rule of 72

A popular shortcut estimates how long it takes for money to double. Divide 72 by the annual growth rate. At an invented rate of 8 per cent, the estimate is nine years. It is only an approximation, but it gives a quick feel for how rate and time interact.

## Compounding works in both directions

The same arithmetic applies to borrowing. Interest on unpaid credit card balances or high-cost loans also compounds, so debts can grow quickly when left unpaid. This is why people often look at the cost of borrowing as carefully as the return on savings.

## Things that reduce the effect

- **Costs and charges.** Fund fees and transaction charges take a share each year, and over long periods this adds up.
- **Taxes.** Tax on interest or gains reduces what is left to compound. Rules change, so check incometax.gov.in for the current position.
- **Inflation.** Growth that merely matches rising prices does not increase real purchasing power.
- **Market ups and downs.** Investments linked to markets do not grow in a straight line, and losses can interrupt compounding.

## Key takeaway

Compounding means earning growth on earlier growth, and its effect depends heavily on time. It is a way of understanding long-term saving, not a promise of any result. This article is educational only.

## In everyday words

"How can small amounts turn into a lot?" The answer is that the money you earn gets added to the pile, and then the bigger pile earns more. Each year the growth is calculated on a slightly larger amount than the year before. The longer it goes on, the more the effect shows, which is why people talk so much about giving money time.
