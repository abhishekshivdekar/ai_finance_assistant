# Reading a Price Chart

A price chart is a picture of how the price of a share or an index has changed over time. Charts compress thousands of trades into shapes that are easy to scan. This article explains the basic parts of a chart in plain terms. It describes how to read them, and it does not claim that any pattern predicts the future.

## The two axes

Time runs along the bottom, from older on the left to newer on the right. Price runs up the side. A line that rises from left to right shows a price that increased over the period, and a line that falls shows a price that decreased.

## Line charts and candlesticks

A **line chart** joins one price per period, usually the closing price. It is simple and good for seeing the overall direction.

A **candlestick chart** shows four prices for each period, such as one day: the open, the high, the low and the close. Each period is drawn as a small box with thin lines, called wicks, above and below it.

- The **body** of the candle spans from the opening price to the closing price.
- A candle is often coloured one way when the close is above the open and another way when it is below.- The **wicks** show the highest and lowest prices touched during the period.

A long wick means the price travelled far from the body during the period and then came back.
## Trend

A **trend** is the general direction of prices over a stretch of time. An uptrend has a series of higher highs and higher lows. A downtrend has lower highs and lower lows. A sideways pattern moves within a range. The trend you see depends on the time window: a stock may be falling over a week and rising over a year.

## Support and resistance

People often describe a price level where the price has repeatedly stopped falling as **support**, and a level where it has repeatedly stopped rising as **resistance**. These are descriptions of past behaviour. A level that held many times can still break.

## Volume

Under many charts there is a set of bars called **volume**, which shows how many shares traded in each period. High volume means more activity. A price move on high volume involved many participants, while a move on low volume involved fewer. Volume gives context to price but does not say what happens next.

## Moving averages

A **moving average** is a line that smooths the price by averaging the last several periods, for example the last 50 days. It helps show the direction by reducing day-to-day noise. Because it uses past prices, it always lags behind the latest move.

## Limits of charts

Charts show what has happened. Two people can look at the same chart and see different stories, and patterns that look convincing in hindsight often fail to repeat. Charts also leave out the reasons behind price moves, such as company earnings or economic news.
## A small invented example

Imagine a daily candle for a share with an open of 200, a high of 208, a low of 196 and a close of 205, all invented numbers. The body goes from 200 to 205, a short upper wick reaches 208, and a longer lower wick reaches 196. It tells you that the price dipped during the day, recovered and ended higher than it started.

## In everyday words

A chart plots price against time, and candlesticks show the open, high, low and close for each period. A trend is the general direction, and volume shows how many shares traded. All of this describes the past and cannot promise what happens next. This article is for education and is not advice.
