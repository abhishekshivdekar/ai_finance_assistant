# Macroeconomic Indicators and Markets

Markets do not move in isolation. They react to the state of the wider economy, and the economy is described by a handful of regularly published numbers called macroeconomic indicators. This article explains four common ones and why financial markets pay attention when they change.

## Inflation

**Inflation** is the general rise in prices of goods and services over time. It is usually reported as the yearly change in a price index, which tracks the cost of a typical basket of items that households buy. When inflation is high, money buys less than before. Central banks watch it closely because very high or unstable inflation harms savers and planning, while very low inflation can signal weak demand.

## Interest rates and RBI policy

An **interest rate** is the price of borrowing money. In India, the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) is the central bank. Its monetary policy committee meets regularly to set the policy rate, often called the repo rate, which is the rate at which the RBI lends to banks. The policy rate influences what banks charge on loans and pay on deposits, so it spreads through the economy.

If the RBI raises the policy rate, borrowing becomes more costly and spending can cool down, which can help control inflation. If it lowers the rate, borrowing becomes cheaper, which can support growth. The RBI publishes its decisions and statements on its website.

## GDP

**Gross domestic product (GDP)** is the total value of goods and services produced in a country in a period. Its growth rate tells us whether the economy is expanding or shrinking. Strong growth often goes along with rising company revenues, while a slowdown can reduce them.

## Other indicators

Employment figures, industrial output, trade balances and the exchange rate also feed into the picture. Governments and central banks publish these on a schedule, so markets know in advance when new numbers are due.

## Why markets react

Share prices depend on expected future company earnings, and indicators change those expectations. Some typical links are:

- **Higher interest rates** can reduce company profits through higher borrowing costs and can make safer interest-paying options look more attractive compared with shares.
- **Falling interest rates** can have the opposite effect.
- **Rising inflation** can squeeze companies that cannot easily pass on costs.
- **Faster GDP growth** can raise expectations for profits.
- **Bond prices** generally move in the opposite direction to interest rates, which is a link covered in bond education.

These links are tendencies, not rules. Different sectors respond differently. A bank, an exporter and a utility company may all react in different ways to the same news.

## Surprises matter most

Because markets look ahead, what matters is often the gap between the published number and what analysts expected. If inflation comes in lower than forecasts, prices may rise even though inflation is still high. If a number matches expectations, the reaction may be small, because the news was already priced in.

## A small invented example

Suppose analysts expect the policy rate to stay unchanged, but the central bank unexpectedly lowers it. Shares of companies with heavy loans might move up, while banks might see mixed reactions. If the cut had been widely expected, prices might hardly move. The scenario is invented and shows the reasoning, not a prediction.

## In everyday words

Inflation, interest rates, RBI policy and GDP describe the health of the economy. Markets react when these numbers change because they alter expectations about company profits and borrowing costs. The reaction depends on how the news compares with what people expected. This article is educational and does not forecast markets.
