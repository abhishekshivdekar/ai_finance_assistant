# How Stock Markets Work

A stock market is an organised place where buyers and sellers trade shares of listed companies. Today almost all of this happens electronically, but the idea is the same as a traditional market: buyers and sellers meet, agree on a price and exchange something of value.

## Exchanges and their role

A stock exchange provides the system on which trades are matched. It also sets listing rules for companies and publishes trading information. In India the two main exchanges are the NSE and the BSE. In the United States, examples include the New York Stock Exchange and Nasdaq. A company must meet an exchange's requirements before its shares can be listed.

## Who takes part

- **Retail investors** are individuals investing their own money.
- **Institutional investors** are large organisations such as mutual funds, pension funds and insurers.
- **Brokers** are licensed firms that place orders on behalf of investors.
- **Market makers and traders** help keep buying and selling active, which adds liquidity.
- **Regulators** such as SEBI oversee the market to protect investors and encourage fair practice.

## Primary and secondary markets

In the **primary market**, a company sells new shares to investors, for example in an initial public offering. The money raised goes to the company. In the **secondary market**, investors trade existing shares with one another through the exchange. The company does not receive money from these later trades, but a liquid secondary market makes people more willing to buy in the first place.

## How a price is set

At any moment, buyers state the highest price they are willing to pay, called the **bid**, and sellers state the lowest price they will accept, called the **ask**. The gap between the two is the **spread**. When a bid and an ask match, a trade happens and that becomes the latest traded price. If more people want to buy than sell, prices tend to rise, and if more want to sell than buy, they tend to fall.

## Types of orders

- A **market order** asks for a trade straight away at the best available price.
- A **limit order** sets a price limit, and it only executes at that price or better, so it may not execute at all.
- A **stop order** becomes an active order only after a chosen price level is reached.

## Trading hours and settlement

Exchanges operate during fixed hours, often with a pre-opening session before the main session. After a trade, a process called **settlement** transfers the shares and the money between the parties. Many markets now settle within a day or two of the trade, and the exact timing is set by market rules.

## Indices: a snapshot of the market

An **index** tracks a group of shares to show how a market or segment is doing. The Nifty 50 and the Sensex are well-known Indian indices, while the S&P 500 is a common United States example. Each index has its own method for choosing and weighting companies, so the number is a summary, not the experience of every stock. Index values are measured in points, not in currency.

## Bull and bear markets

Investors often describe a prolonged period of rising prices as a **bull market** and a prolonged fall as a **bear market**. These labels are informal, and nobody can reliably forecast when one phase will turn into the other. Markets have historically seen both, and short-term moves can be large.

## Market hours versus news

Prices often react quickly to news such as company results, policy announcements or global events. Investors sometimes say that a market is "pricing in" expectations, meaning that prices already reflect what most participants believe will happen.

## Key takeaway

Stock markets bring buyers and sellers together under rules that aim to be transparent. Prices come from the interaction of bids and asks, and indices summarise broad movements. This article is educational and does not predict market direction or suggest any trade.
