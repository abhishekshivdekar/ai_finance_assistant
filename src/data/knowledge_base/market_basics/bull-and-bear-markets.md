# Bull and Bear Markets

News reports often say that "the bulls are in charge" or that "the bears have taken over". These phrases describe the general mood and direction of a market. This article explains what the words mean, how people use them, and why they are descriptions of the past rather than predictions.

## Where the names come from

A bull attacks by thrusting its horns upward, and a bear swipes its paws downward. Traders borrowed the pictures. A **bull market** is a stretch of time in which prices across a market are mostly rising and confidence is high. A **bear market** is a stretch in which prices are mostly falling and caution is high.

## Is there an exact definition?

There is no official rule. Many people use a rough convention: a fall of about one fifth from a recent peak is called a bear market, and a rise of about one fifth from a recent low is called a bull market. Others use different thresholds or look at how long the move lasts. Because the labels are informal, two commentators can disagree about whether a market is currently bull or bear.

A shorter dip of around a tenth from a peak is often called a **correction**. Corrections are more common than bear markets and usually end sooner, but only afterwards can anyone say how far a fall went.

## What usually goes along with each phase

In a bull market, company profits are often growing, jobs are plentiful, and more people feel willing to invest. Prices can keep rising for years. In a bear market, profits may be shrinking, the economy may be slowing, and fear can lead people to leave the market, which pushes prices lower still.

These are tendencies, not laws. Markets have risen during weak economies and fallen during strong ones, because prices reflect what participants expect about the future, not only what is happening today.

## Markets move in cycles, but not on a timetable

Looking at long price histories, both bull and bear phases appear again and again. Bull markets have tended to last longer than bear markets, and bear markets have often been sharper. Yet no one can reliably say when a phase will start or end. Even professionals who study the data full time regularly misjudge turning points.

An invented example makes the point. Imagine an index that climbs from 1,000 to 1,500 points over three years, drops to 1,150 in a year, and then rises again. Anyone looking only at the first three years would call it a bull market, and anyone looking only at the fall would call it a bear market. The label depends on the window you choose.

## Why the labels matter for understanding

Knowing the terms helps you read financial news without confusion. It also reminds you that markets do not move in a straight line. Both rises and falls are part of how markets have behaved, which is one reason that risk, time horizon and diversification are common topics in investing education.

The labels also carry emotion. Words like "crash" and "boom" can make a normal movement sound dramatic, so it helps to look at actual numbers, such as how far an index has moved and over how long.

## In everyday words

A bull market is a long stretch of mostly rising prices, and a bear market is a long stretch of mostly falling prices. The names are informal, and nobody agrees on one exact definition. Markets have gone through both kinds of phase, and no one can predict the timing in advance. This article is for education only and does not suggest any action.
