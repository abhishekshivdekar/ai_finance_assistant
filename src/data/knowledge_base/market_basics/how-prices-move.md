# How Prices Move in a Market

The price of a share changes many times during a trading day. There is no one person who decides the number. Prices move because of the actions of thousands of buyers and sellers, and those actions are driven by supply, demand and information.

## Supply and demand in a share

Every listed share has a limited number of units that holders are willing to sell at a given moment. That is the supply. The number of people who want to own the share at that moment is the demand. When more people want to own the share than are willing to part with it, buyers compete and are ready to pay more, so the price tends to rise. When more holders want to sell than there are buyers, sellers have to accept less, so the price tends to fall.

At the exchange, this contest shows up as a list of orders. Buyers post the prices they will pay and sellers post the prices they will accept. A trade happens when a buyer and seller agree, and that agreed amount becomes the latest price.

## Why demand and supply change

Many things can shift how much buyers and sellers want to trade:

- **Company news.** Quarterly results, new products, changes in management or a legal case can change what people think the business is worth.
- **Industry and economy.** A change in interest rates, fuel costs or consumer spending can affect whole groups of companies at once.
- **Global events.** Overseas markets, currency moves and political events can affect sentiment in other countries.
- **Money flows.** Large funds moving money in or out of a market can push prices, even when nothing has changed about the companies.
- **Mood and expectations.** Fear and excitement spread between people, and prices can move further than the facts alone would suggest.

## The role of expectations

Markets look forward. A share price is not only a measure of what a company earns today. It reflects what participants collectively expect it to earn in the future. This is why a company can report higher profits and still see its price fall: if the market expected even more, the news is a disappointment. Investors sometimes say the good news was already "priced in".

## Why prices jump on news

When surprising news arrives, many people try to act at the same moment. Orders pile up on one side of the book, and the price can move quickly until buyers and sellers are balanced again. Thin trading, meaning fewer participants, can make these jumps larger, which is why smaller or less actively traded companies often show bigger swings.

## Short-term noise versus longer-term drivers

Over minutes or days, prices can be pushed around by news, rumours and the habits of traders. Over many years, prices have tended to follow the growth of company earnings, though not in a straight line and with no guarantee. This difference explains why short-term movements are hard to explain even for experts, while long-term trends are easier to discuss in hindsight.

## A small invented example

Suppose a company's shares are quoted at 100 rupees, which is an invented number. News arrives that a rival is launching a cheaper product. Some holders decide they want to sell, so they accept 98, then 96. Buyers notice and hold back, waiting for lower offers. The price drifts down until some buyers decide that the new price is attractive and begin to buy again.
## In everyday words

A share price moves because more people want to buy than sell, or the other way round. News, expectations, big money flows and mood all change that balance. Prices already include what most people expect, so surprises cause the biggest moves. This article is educational and does not predict any price.
