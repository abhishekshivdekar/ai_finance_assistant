# Volatility Explained

Volatility describes how much and how quickly an investment's price moves. The article "Risk and Diversification Basics" gives a short definition and mentions drawdown. This article looks at how volatility is measured, how to read the numbers, and how it affects the experience of investing.

## What volatility looks like

A calm investment drifts up or down slowly. A volatile one can jump several percent in a day and reverse the next. Over a year, both could end at the same price, yet the second would have been far more uncomfortable to hold.

## Measuring it

The most common measure is **standard deviation**, which describes how far returns typically stray from their average. A higher figure means wider swings.

Here is an invented example. Suppose an imaginary fund has an average yearly return of 10 percent and a standard deviation of 15 percentage points. As a rough guide, about two years out of three the return would fall within 15 points either side of the average, between -5 and +25 percent. A steadier fund with a standard deviation of 5 points would usually land between 5 and 15 percent. These numbers are made up to show how to read the measure.

Standard deviation treats upward and downward moves alike. Many people care mainly about the downward ones, so other measures exist too.

## Related ideas

- **Drawdown.** The fall from a peak to a later low. A drawdown of 30 percent means the value dropped by nearly a third before recovering.
- **Beta.** How much an investment tends to move compared with the overall market. A beta above one means larger swings than the market.
- **Volatility index.** Some exchanges publish an index estimating expected market volatility. In India, the NSE publishes one, often called India VIX, and it is sometimes described as a fear gauge.

## The recovery arithmetic

Losses are harder to recover from than they look. After a 50 percent fall, an investment must rise by 100 percent to get back to where it began. Using invented figures, 100 rupees falling to 50 needs to double to return to 100. Large drawdowns therefore carry an extra burden, and this is one reason high volatility matters.

## Volatility versus risk

Volatility is not exactly the same as permanent loss. A share price can swing widely and then recover, or it can fall and never return. Still, volatility matters in practice for two reasons. First, big drops may force a sale at a bad time if the money is needed. Second, large swings can be hard to live with, and investors sometimes abandon a plan at the worst moment.

## What affects volatility

- News, earnings and economic data.
- Company size, since smaller companies often swing more, as the article "Large-Cap, Mid-Cap and Small-Cap Companies" notes.
- Investor mood, which can amplify moves.
- How easily the asset trades.

## Managing its effect

Investors often respond with a longer time horizon, a mix of asset classes, regular investing and an allocation they can tolerate. None removes volatility, but together they can make it easier to live with.

## Limits of the measures

Volatility figures are based on the past. Calm periods can end abruptly, and extreme events are more common than a simple bell-curve picture would suggest.

## Key takeaway

Volatility measures how widely an investment's price swings, usually through standard deviation. It is not identical to risk, but it shapes how bearable an investment is. This article is educational only.

## In everyday words

Some stocks are jumpy. One week the price shoots up, and the next week it drops back down, and it can feel like a rollercoaster. People use the word volatility for how big and how fast these ups and downs are. A calm stock moves a little each day, while a jumpy one can move a lot, and neither one tells you where the price will go next.
