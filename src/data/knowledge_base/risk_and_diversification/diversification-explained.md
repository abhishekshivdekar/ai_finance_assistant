# Diversification Explained

The article "Risk and Diversification Basics" lists several ways to spread money around. This article focuses on a single question: how many holdings are enough, and what does adding more actually achieve? The answer shows both the power and the limits of the idea.

## The mathematics in plain words

Each company has its own ups and downs. Some of those come from events that hit only that firm, such as a factory fire or a management scandal. Others come from forces that affect the whole market, such as an economic slowdown.

When money is split across many companies, the firm-specific events tend to cancel out. One firm has bad news while another has good news, so the total moves less. The market-wide movements do not cancel, because they push every holding in the same direction.

So diversification removes one kind of risk, the firm-specific kind, and leaves the other.

## An invented illustration

Suppose a company has a one in twenty chance of losing half its value in a given year because of its own problems. These figures are invented.

- An investor holding only that company faces a one in twenty chance of a 50 percent loss on the whole amount.
- An investor holding twenty similar, unrelated companies would typically see about one of them suffer such a loss. Since each makes up one twentieth of the total, the damage to the whole is around 2.5 percent.

The chance that something goes wrong is similar. What changes is how much any one failure matters.

## Diminishing returns

The first few holdings reduce firm-specific risk a lot. Adding more helps less each time. Beyond a certain number, extra holdings may do little except add complexity. Researchers have found that much of the benefit arrives within a few dozen well-chosen holdings, although the exact number depends on how different the holdings are from each other. ## Counting holdings is not enough

Ten holdings are not necessarily ten sources of protection. If all ten are banks, a problem in the banking sector could hit them together. Real diversification depends on how differently the holdings behave, a point the article "Correlation Basics" explains. Likewise, owning three funds that each hold the same large companies may add little.

## Diversification through funds

For many people, a fund is a way to own a large number of holdings without choosing each one. A broad index fund can spread money across many companies at once, though it is still exposed to the market as a whole. Investors often look at a fund's top holdings and sector breakdown, listed in its factsheet, to see how widely it is spread.

## Over-diversification

Spreading too thinly has costs. Many small holdings can be hard to track, may add fees, and can make a portfolio behave like the overall market while still charging for management. Some investors regard this as diluting the effect of their strongest convictions, while others see it as an acceptable price for safety. The answer depends on goals and temperament.

## What diversification cannot do

- It cannot remove market-wide risk.
- It cannot guarantee a profit.
- It cannot prevent losses in a broad downturn, when many assets fall together.
- It does not replace understanding what each holding is.

## Key takeaway

Diversification works by letting company-specific problems offset each other, with the greatest benefit coming from the first few unrelated holdings. It leaves market risk untouched. This article is educational only.
