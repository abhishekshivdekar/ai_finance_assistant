# Correlation Basics

Correlation measures how closely two investments move in relation to each other. The article "Risk and Diversification Basics" introduces it in a short paragraph. This article explains how the measure works, how to read it, and why it determines whether a mix of investments actually smooths a portfolio.

## The scale from minus one to plus one

Correlation is expressed as a number between -1 and +1.

- **+1** means two investments move in perfect step. When one rises, so does the other, by a proportional amount.
- **0** means no consistent relationship. One's movement tells you little about the other.
- **-1** means they move in exactly opposite directions. When one rises, the other falls.

Real investments rarely sit at the extremes. Two shares in the same industry may have a correlation of 0.7 or 0.8, while a share and a government bond might be close to zero or slightly negative in some periods. These are general illustrations, not measured values for any actual pair.

## An invented example

Consider two imaginary investments over four years, with yearly changes shown in percent.

| Year | Umbrella seller | Ice cream seller |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | +10 | -4 |
| 2 | -6 | +12 |
| 3 | +8 | -2 |
| 4 | -3 | +9 |

When the first does well the second tends to do worse, and vice versa. They have a strongly negative correlation in this made-up table. Holding both would give a smoother combined result than holding either alone, because their swings partly offset each other.

If both moved up and down together, combining them would give almost no smoothing.

## Why it matters for diversification

Diversification benefits depend on correlation, not on the number of holdings. The lower the correlation between holdings, the more their ups and downs cancel out. The article "Diversification Explained" describes how many holdings can still fail to protect if they are all closely correlated.

## Correlation is not fixed

Relationships between assets shift over time. Shares and bonds have sometimes moved in opposite directions and sometimes fallen together, for instance when rising interest rates hurt both. Measured correlation also depends on the period used and the data frequency.

## Correlations tend to rise in a crisis

A well-known observation is that in severe market stress, many risky assets fall together, so correlations that looked low in calm times can jump. Diversification still helps, but it may protect less in the worst moments than historical figures suggest.

## Correlation versus cause

A high correlation does not mean one investment causes the other to move. Two things may both respond to a third factor, such as the economy, or the pattern may be a coincidence.

## How investors use it

- To check whether the funds in a portfolio overlap in what they hold.
- To compare asset classes, such as shares, bonds and gold, when thinking about allocation, as in "Asset Allocation Explained".
- To judge whether a new addition adds real variety or just more of the same.

## Limits

Correlation looks backwards and captures only the typical direction, not how large the moves are. It is one tool among several and cannot predict how assets will behave in the future.

## Key takeaway

Correlation tells investors whether two holdings tend to move together, and diversification works best when they do not. The relationship changes over time and can tighten in a crisis. This article is educational only.
