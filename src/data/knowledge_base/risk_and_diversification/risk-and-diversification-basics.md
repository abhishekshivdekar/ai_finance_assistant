# Risk and Diversification Basics

Every investment carries some risk, which in finance means the chance that the actual outcome differs from what was expected, including the chance of losing money. Understanding the kinds of risk, and how spreading money around can soften their impact, is one of the core ideas of investing.

## Risk and return

Investors often observe that higher potential returns usually come with higher risk. A savings account in a regulated bank has little price risk but typically offers modest returns. Shares of a young company might grow quickly, but their prices can swing sharply or fall to zero. This trade-off is often called the risk-return relationship, and no investment offers high returns without the possibility of loss.

## Common types of risk

- **Market risk.** The chance that an entire market falls, pulling down most investments within it.
- **Company-specific risk.** Problems that affect one business, such as poor management, a failed product or a scandal.
- **Interest rate risk.** Changes in interest rates can affect bond prices and borrowing costs.
- **Inflation risk.** Rising prices can erode the purchasing power of money kept in low-return holdings.
- **Currency risk.** For investments held in a foreign currency, exchange rate movements can raise or lower returns when converted back.
- **Liquidity risk.** Some assets cannot be sold quickly without accepting a lower price.

## Volatility

Volatility describes how much an investment's price moves up and down over time. A volatile asset has big swings. Volatility is not exactly the same as risk of permanent loss, but it matters because large drops can be hard to live with and may force someone to sell at a poor time. Investors often talk about **drawdown**, the fall from a peak to a later low, to describe how painful a bad stretch can be.

## What is diversification?

Diversification means spreading money across different investments so that one bad result does not do too much damage. The familiar saying is not to put all your eggs in one basket. If an investor holds one company and it fails, the loss is total. If the money is spread across many companies, the failure of one has a much smaller effect.

## Ways to diversify

- **Across companies.** Holding many different shares instead of one or two.
- **Across industries.** Banking, technology, healthcare and consumer goods often respond differently to the same events.
- **Across asset classes.** Mixing shares, bonds, gold and cash, which tend not to move in perfect step.
- **Across regions.** Combining domestic and international holdings.
- **Across time.** Investing smaller amounts regularly rather than a single lump sum, which spreads out the effect of price changes.

## Correlation: why the mix matters

Two assets are **correlated** when they tend to move together. Diversification works best when holdings do not all move in the same direction at the same time. Owning ten technology companies spreads company risk, but they may still fall together if the technology sector struggles. Mixing different types of assets can reduce that effect.

## Limits of diversification

Diversification reduces company-specific risk, but it cannot remove market risk. In a broad downturn, many assets can fall together. It also does not guarantee a profit or protect against all losses. Holding too many overlapping funds can create a false sense of safety when the underlying holdings are similar.

## Risk tolerance and capacity

Investors often distinguish between **risk tolerance**, how comfortable someone feels with ups and downs, and **risk capacity**, how much loss their situation can realistically absorb. A longer time horizon typically allows more time to recover from falls, while money needed soon is often kept in steadier places.

## Key takeaway

Risk cannot be avoided, but it can be understood and spread out. Diversification is a widely used tool for reducing the impact of any single failure. This article is educational and does not assess any individual's situation.
