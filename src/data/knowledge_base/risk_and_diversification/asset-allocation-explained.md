# Asset Allocation Explained

Asset allocation means deciding how a total pool of money is divided among broad categories, called asset classes. The article "Risk and Diversification Basics" mentions mixing shares, bonds, gold and cash in one line. This article looks at the allocation decision itself, because many investors consider it more influential than the choice of any single holding.

## The main asset classes

- **Equities (shares).** Ownership in companies. They offer higher potential growth and larger swings.
- **Debt (bonds, deposits and similar).** Lending money for interest. Usually steadier, but with credit and interest rate risks.
- **Gold.** Often held as a store of value that may move differently from shares.
- **Cash and cash-like holdings.** Very stable and easy to use, but the potential growth is lowest.
- **Real estate and others.** Less liquid and covered less in this knowledge base.

Each class behaves differently when the economy changes, which is why combining them can smooth the journey.

## Why allocation matters

Imagine two investors who both choose good companies, but one keeps nearly everything in equities and the other holds a mix of equities and debt. When shares fall sharply, the first investor's total drops far more. The difference comes from the split, not from the stock selection. Academic studies have suggested that the broad mix explains a large part of the variation in portfolio results over time, although how large a part is debated <!-- VERIFY: academic findings on the share of portfolio variation explained by asset allocation -->.

## Factors that shape an allocation

Investors often consider:

- **Time horizon.** Money needed in two years is often treated differently from money needed in twenty.
- **Goal.** Saving for a home deposit differs from long-term retirement saving. The article "Setting Financial Goals and Planning for Retirement" covers goals.
- **Risk tolerance.** How comfortable someone is seeing their balance fall.
- **Risk capacity.** How much loss their finances could absorb without harming essential plans.
- **Income stability.** A steady salary lets some people take more variation than an uncertain income would.

## An invented example

Here are three imaginary mixes, described only to show how allocations differ. They are not recommendations.

| Style | Equities | Debt | Gold or other |
|---|---|---|---|
| More cautious | 30 percent | 60 percent | 10 percent |
| Balanced | 60 percent | 30 percent | 10 percent |
| Growth-oriented | 80 percent | 15 percent | 5 percent |

A cautious mix typically swings less, with a lower growth potential. A growth-oriented mix typically offers more potential growth along with larger falls. None is correct for all people.

## Rules of thumb

Some people use simple formulas, such as subtracting their age from a fixed number to set an equity share. These are shortcuts that ignore personal circumstances, and they are widely debated. Treat them as starting points for thinking rather than answers.

## Allocation changes over time

As a goal gets closer, many investors gradually reduce the share of volatile assets. Life events such as a job change, a new dependant or inheritance can also shift the picture. Over time, price movements change the mix without any action, which is why the article "Rebalancing Explained" matters.

## Key takeaway

Asset allocation is the high-level split between shares, debt, gold and cash, shaped by time horizon, goals and comfort with risk. It is one of the main drivers of how a portfolio behaves. This article is educational and offers no personal recommendation.
