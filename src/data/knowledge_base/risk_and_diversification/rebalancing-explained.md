# Rebalancing Explained

Rebalancing means bringing a portfolio back to its intended mix after market moves have pushed it away. The article "Asset Allocation Explained" describes how a mix is chosen. This article is about keeping it in shape over time, which is a separate habit from choosing it.

## Why portfolios drift

Suppose an investor starts with a 60 percent share and 40 percent debt split. Over a strong year for shares, the share part grows faster. The mix may become 70 percent shares and 30 percent debt without the investor doing anything. The portfolio now carries more risk than the one originally chosen.

The reverse also happens. After a fall in shares, the share portion shrinks, and the portfolio becomes more cautious than planned.

## An invented example

An investor holds 1,00,000 rupees, split 60,000 in shares and 40,000 in debt. These numbers are invented.

After a year, shares rise 20 percent and debt rises 5 percent. The shares are now 72,000 and the debt is 42,000, a total of 1,14,000. The share part is about 63 percent instead of 60. To return to the original mix, the investor would need shares worth 68,400 and debt worth 45,600. That means moving 3,600 rupees from shares to debt, either by selling some shares and adding to debt, or by directing new money towards debt.

## Common ways to rebalance

- **Calendar-based.** Reviewing at set times, such as once a year, and adjusting if needed.
- **Threshold-based.** Acting only when an asset class drifts beyond a set band, for example five percentage points from target.
- **Using new money.** Directing fresh contributions to the part that has fallen behind, which avoids selling anything.
- **Using fund features.** Some hybrid funds and target-style funds rebalance internally, so the investor does not need to.

Frequent rebalancing can raise costs, and each approach has trade-offs.

## Costs and taxes

Selling an investment to rebalance may trigger transaction costs, exit loads in mutual funds, or tax on gains. These can reduce the benefit of rebalancing, so investors often weigh them. For tax rules, the official source is incometax.gov.in, and the article "Mutual Fund Exit Loads and Lock-ins" covers exit loads.

## What rebalancing does and does not do

It keeps risk close to the level the investor intended. It also has a built-in discipline: it tends to trim what has recently risen and add to what has lagged. It does not guarantee higher returns. In a long, steady rise in shares, a rebalanced portfolio may earn less than one left alone, because it holds fewer shares along the way. In a bumpy market it may help. The purpose is risk control rather than return.

## Behavioural challenge

Rebalancing asks people to act against recent momentum, trimming winners and adding to laggards, which can feel uncomfortable. A written plan with a clear schedule or rule is one way investors try to manage that feeling.

## When to review the target itself

Rebalancing assumes the target mix is still right. Changes in goals, income, time horizon or family situation may call for rethinking the target, not only restoring it.

## Key takeaway

Rebalancing restores a portfolio to its planned mix after prices push it off course, mainly to keep risk where the investor wanted it. It brings costs and does not promise higher returns. This article is educational only.

## In everyday words

Imagine you planned to keep your savings split in a certain way, and then one holding did really well. Now it makes up a bigger share of everything you own than you intended, so your money is leaning more on that one thing. Rebalancing is simply the idea of moving the mix back toward the original plan. It is like straightening a picture that has slowly tilted on the wall.
